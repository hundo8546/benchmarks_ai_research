\begin{abstract}
With the rapid proliferation of AI-generated multimedia content, the growing need for reliable detection of AI-generated media, including deepfakes and misinformation, has been largely unmet. Uniformly applying large vision-language models (VLMs) to all content can be prohibitively expensive. In this work, we formulate frame-level detection as a model selection problem over a set of complementary detectors. We construct a selective-computation cascade in which lightweight detectors process all inputs and a stronger verifier is invoked only for disagreement cases. Disagreement between an artifact-based and a semantic detector flags inputs on which a cheap detector is unreliable, including under shift to unseen generator families. In-domain, a well-trained semantic probe is best without escalation. Under deployment shift to SDXL and FLUX, escalating disagreements to GPT-5.5 recovers up to 7.9 percentage points over a stale semantic probe at 42--51\% of the cost of escalating every input, short of the 97--100\% that uniform escalation reaches. The gain persists, at 3.9--7.1 points, when real and fake images are size-matched to remove a resolution shortcut in the benchmark. Under a more distant BigGAN shift escalation does not help, so its value depends on verifier strength and distributional distance. BigGAN's near-ceiling artifact-detector accuracy is itself largely a native-resolution shortcut. A validation gate with a prespecified significance test reaches at least 90\% agreement with the held-out test oracle in 21 of 33 eligible configurations at $N=100$ and 25 of 33 at $N=200$, and cuts harmful escalations by an order of magnitude.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The rapid advancement of generative models for images and videos has significantly increased the realism and accessibility of synthetic media. Diffusion models~\cite{Wang_2023_ICCV}, GAN-based generators~\cite{wang2020cnn}, and multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can now generate highly convincing content with minimal effort. Although this opens up useful applications, it also raises concerns about misinformation, impersonation, and fraud. As a result, reliably detecting AI-generated media has become an important problem that still needs to be addressed.

One of the main challenges is generalization. Detection models often perform well on the generator families they were trained on, but accuracy degrades significantly when applied to new or unseen generators~\cite{NEURIPS2023_f4d4a021}. Artifact-based detectors exploit low-level frequency and synthesis patterns introduced during generation, while semantic models capture higher-level structural representations. These two signal types fail in different ways, suggesting that combining them provides more robust coverage than either approach alone.


In practical deployment settings, compute cost is a significant concern. High-capacity
vision-language models (VLMs) provide strong reasoning capabilities but are expensive to apply at scale~\cite{chen2024frugalgpt}. Applying these models uniformly to every input is often infeasible in real-time or large-scale systems. Existing approaches typically apply a
fixed detector or ensemble without adapting computation to input difficulty. The central challenge is identifying which inputs require expensive verification rather than applying a
uniform computation to all inputs.


In this work, we address this challenge by modeling frame-level AI-generated media detection as a model selection problem over complementary detectors. We find that disagreement between an artifact-based detector and a semantic detector flags inputs on which at least one cheap detector is unreliable. In-domain, predictions on which the detectors agree are highly reliable. Under generator shift the agreement path degrades, and whether escalating the disagreements actually helps depends on the verifier, which takes a small labeled calibration set to establish.

Unlike approaches built on calibrated confidence scores or learned routing policies, the routing rule itself needs no threshold and no trained parameters, and under distribution shift it performs close to a calibrated confidence rule. We design a cascade that routes inputs based on a signal where lightweight detectors process all inputs, and a stronger verifier is invoked only on disagreement cases. When the semantic probe is trained in-domain, it already achieves strong performance without VLM escalation. The cascade's primary value is under deployment shift, where it recovers
accuracy over a stale semantic probe while avoiding the majority of verifier invocations. This reframes the detection problem as identifying which inputs require expensive computation, rather than uniformly increasing model complexity.

\subsection{Research Questions}

This work is guided by the following research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} Can we reliably estimate which inputs require more powerful models
    for AI-generated media detection, including under generator distribution shift?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} Can such estimates be used to guide model selection across heterogeneous detectors without relying on calibrated confidence scores?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} Does disagreement-based routing reduce verifier cost while recovering accuracy under deployment shift to unseen generator families?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}

This work makes the following contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{C1:} We show that disagreement between artifact-based and semantic detectors flags inputs on which a cheap detector is unreliable, in-domain and under generator shift, although errors both detectors share rise 4--8$\times$ under shift.

    \item \textbf{C2:} We evaluate a simple routing rule that escalates disagreements to a verifier, with no confidence threshold or trained policy.

    \item \textbf{C3:} On SDXL and FLUX, escalating disagreements to GPT-5.5 recovers up to 7.9 percentage points over a stale semantic probe while cutting verifier invocations by 58--64\%, and the gain holds when real and fake images are size-matched.

    \item \textbf{C4:} We show that a learned escalation classifier converges to the disagreement rule, and that a calibrated confidence rule gives a similar gain under shift, so disagreement is the simplest signal rather than the most accurate one.

    \item \textbf{C5:} We introduce a verifier validation gate that uses a significance test on a small labeled sample to decide whether to enable escalation. On held-out splits it agrees with the oracle decision in 25 of 33 configurations at $N{=}200$.

    \item \textbf{C6:} We show that BigGAN's near-ceiling artifact-detector accuracy is largely a native-resolution shortcut, and that SDXL/FLUX carries a similar one (a size-only classifier reaches 98.9--100.0\%), so we also report size-matched results.
\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work}
This section reviews prior work related to AI-generated media detection and adaptive inference. We first identify gaps in existing approaches that motivate this work, then discuss detection methods under distribution shift, examine approaches for selective computation, and finally compare routing strategies based on confidence and learned policies.








\subsection{Gaps in Existing Approaches}

Existing forensic detection and cost-aware inference literature leaves several gaps that motivate this work.

\textbf{Single signal reliance.} Most forensic detectors rely on a single signal type, such as diffusion-based artifacts or reconstruction inconsistencies~\cite{Corvi_2023_ICASSP} or semantic embeddings from large pretrained models~\cite{cozzolino2023raising}. These approaches work well within specific generator families but degrade under generator distribution shift. Recent large-scale evaluations show that no single detector consistently generalizes across modern image generation pipelines~\cite{epstein2023online, cozzolino2023raising}.

\textbf{Confidence-based routing under shift.} Cost-aware inference methods and selective classification approaches rely on calibrated confidence estimates to decide when to defer or escalate predictions. Predictive uncertainty in deep models degrades significantly under distribution shift~\cite{ovadia2019can}, so confidence thresholds fit on one distribution may not transfer to the regimes where robust detection is most needed. Existing literature on uncertainty estimation and selective prediction does not examine this question for heterogeneous detector cascades.

\textbf{Disagreement as a routing signal.} Inter-model disagreement has been studied extensively in active learning~\cite{seung1992query} and ensemble-based uncertainty estimation~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017simple}. In these settings, disagreement identifies informative samples or improves predictive uncertainty. Prior work treats disagreement as a mechanism for aggregation or data selection. Its role as a routing signal for cost-aware inference remains unexplored in forensic detection pipelines. Specifically, using disagreement as a diagnostic to indicate when low-cost detectors should not be trusted has not been studied.

This work addresses these gaps in four ways. First, we combine heterogeneous artifact-based and semantic signals through a disagreement rule with no threshold or trained parameters. Second, we compare disagreement with a calibrated joint-confidence rule at matched operating points across multiple generator families, and find that it performs close to that rule, ahead in-domain and tied or behind under shift, while needing no threshold or probability outputs. Third, we account for both latency and token cost when evaluating VLM-based escalation strategies. Fourth, we reinterpret disagreement as a diagnostic for selective invocation of more expensive models, not as a voting mechanism.

\textbf{Dataset shortcuts.} Detectors trained on GenImage-family benchmarks can exploit JPEG compression and image-size biases rather than generative artifacts, because real and synthetic images in these benchmarks are acquired and processed differently~\cite{grommelt2024fake}. We measure this for our artifact detector on BigGAN (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) and repeat the SDXL/FLUX deployment-shift evaluation with real and fake images size-matched (Section~\ref{sec:sizematched}).

\subsection{Detection under Distribution Shift}

A large body of work has focused on detecting AI-generated images using artifact-based features. Early approaches such as CNN-based detectors~\cite{wang2020cnn} exploit low-level patterns introduced by generative models. More recent methods aim to improve generalization across generators, such as universal fake detectors~\cite{ojha2023fakedetect}. Benchmark datasets such as GenImage~\cite{NEURIPS2023_f4d4a021} highlight the difficulty of this problem, showing that many detectors degrade significantly when evaluated on unseen generators.

Other approaches leverage higher-level representations. Models such as CLIP~\cite{pmlr-v139-radford21a} learn semantic features from large-scale image-text data and have been used in downstream detection tasks. Diffusion-specific detection methods, such as DIRE~\cite{Wang_2023_ICCV}, further illustrate the diversity of signals used in modern detection systems. Despite these advances, no single model consistently generalizes across generator families, motivating the use of multiple complementary detectors.

Frequency-domain analysis provides a complementary artifact signal. Spectral methods
exploit periodic and structured artifacts introduced by GAN upsampling and diffusion
denoising pipelines, and have shown strong in-domain generalization~\cite{Corvi_2023_ICASSP}.
Beyond CLIP, recent vision encoders such as DINOv2~\cite{oquab2023dinov2} and
SigLIP2~\cite{tschannen2025siglip2} offer stronger visual representations and have
demonstrated improved robustness under limited training data. Their behavior under
generator distribution shift in forensic detection tasks remains underexplored, motivating
their inclusion as semantic detector candidates in this work.


\subsection{Selective Computation and Model Cascades}

Selective computation has been studied as a way to reduce inference cost by adapting computation to input difficulty. Early work on selective classification~\cite{NIPS2017_4a8423d5} proposes abstaining on uncertain predictions to improve reliability. More recent systems extend this idea to model selection, routing inputs across models of varying capacity. For example, FrugalGPT~\cite{chen2024frugalgpt} reduces cost by selecting among large language models based on estimated utility. These approaches typically rely on confidence scores or calibrated probabilities to guide routing decisions. However, such signals can be unreliable under distribution shift, where models may produce overconfident incorrect predictions.

\subsection{Routing Signals for Model Selection}

Most existing routing strategies use confidence-based signals, such as entropy or prediction probability, to determine whether additional computation is required. While effective in controlled settings, these signals often degrade under distribution shift due to poor calibration. Learned routing policies, such as bandit-based approaches, attempt to improve decision-making by training a model to predict the best action
from input features.

In contrast, this work explores disagreement between heterogeneous detectors as a routing signal. Rather than relying on calibrated confidence scores or learned policies, we use prediction disagreement between artifact-based and semantic models to select inputs for escalation. This gives a simple signal with no threshold to fit; we compare it directly against a calibrated confidence rule under distribution shift.

\section{Proposed Solution}
This section describes the proposed framework for difficulty-aware model selection. We first formalize the routing problem and define the cost model, then introduce the disagreement-based routing strategy, examine the alternative routing baselines used for comparison, and finally detail how latency is measured across routing strategies.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

We consider the problem of detecting AI-generated media using a set of heterogeneous detectors with different computational costs and failure modes. Given an input sample $x$, the goal is to select an appropriate subset of detectors to apply such that prediction accuracy is maintained while minimizing computational cost.

Let:
\[
\mathcal{M} = \{f_{\text{art}}, f_{\text{sem}}, f_{\text{vlm}}\}
\]
denote the set of available detectors, corresponding to an artifact-based model, a semantic model, and a high-capacity vision-language model. Each model produces a binary prediction $\hat{y}_i \in \{0,1\}$. A routing policy $\pi(x) \in \{0,1\}$ determines whether the VLM is invoked on input $x$, with the artifact and semantic detectors always applied as
low-cost first-stage filters. We evaluate multiple instantiations of this framework in which the artifact detector is instantiated as a dual-stream FFT probe (primary) or CNNSpot
(baseline), and the semantic detector as CLIP, SigLIP2, or DINOv2.

We report on two complementary cost metrics. Wall-clock latency, measured in milliseconds on a fixed GPU, applies to every detection stage:
\[
C_{\text{lat}}(x, \pi) = \kappa^{\text{lat}}_{\text{art}} + \kappa^{\text{lat}}_{\text{sem}}
+ \mathbbm{1}[\pi(x)=1] \cdot \kappa^{\text{lat}}_{\text{vlm}}(x),
\]
where $\kappa^{\text{lat}}_{\text{vlm}}(x)$ is measured per sample because VLM latency depends on input resolution. The token cost, measured as mean VLM input and output tokens per sample, applies only when the VLM is invoked and reflects the standard unit of cost for commercial VLM APIs~\cite{chen2024frugalgpt}:
\[
C_{\text{tok}}(x, \pi) = \mathbbm{1}[\pi(x)=1] \cdot \bigl(\tau^{\text{in}}_{\text{vlm}}(x)
+ \tau^{\text{out}}_{\text{vlm}}(x)\bigr).
\]
The routing objective is to minimize the expected cost under either metric while maintaining a target accuracy $\alpha$:
\[
\min_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_x[C(x, \pi)] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \text{Accuracy}(\pi) \geq \alpha.
\]
We report $C_{\text{lat}}$ for every evaluated method and $C_{\text{tok}}$ wherever the verifier is an API model (GPT-5.5; Section~\ref{sec:cost_accounting}), as the deployment scenarios vary between self-hosted (latency-bound) and API-based (token-bound) inference.

\subsection{Disagreement as a Routing Signal}

We flag inputs for escalation using disagreement between the artifact-based and semantic detectors:

\begin{equation}
d(x) = \mathbbm{1}\left[\hat{y}_{\text{art}}(x) \neq \hat{y}_{\text{sem}}(x)\right].
\label{eq:disagree}
\end{equation}

In-domain, predictions on which the detectors agree are highly reliable. Disagreement
corresponds to cases where predictions conflict, indicating that at least one detector
is wrong and that additional computation may be required. Unlike confidence-based
measures such as entropy, this signal needs no threshold. It works because artifact and
semantic detectors tend to fail on different inputs. That complementarity weakens under
distribution shift: errors on which both detectors agree, which disagreement cannot
flag, rise 4--8$\times$ on SDXL/FLUX (Section~\ref{sec:validation_gate}).


\subsection{Disagreement-Based Routing}

Using the disagreement signal, we define a simple routing policy:

\begin{equation}
\pi_{\text{disagree}}(x) =
\begin{cases}
0, & \text{if } d(x) = 0 \\
1, & \text{if } d(x) = 1
\end{cases}
\label{eq:pidisagree}
\end{equation}

where $\pi(x) = 1$ indicates that the VLM is invoked. Inputs where the detectors agree are handled by the cheap stage alone while disagreement cases are escalated to the VLM. This enables selective allocation of computation based on disagreement. Note that outputting the
semantic detector's prediction on all inputs is equivalent to this routing policy without VLM escalation, since the artifact and semantic detectors produce identical predictions on
agreement cases by definition. In-domain, this semantic-only baseline achieves the best
cost-accuracy tradeoff. The cascade's value is under deployment shift, where a stale
semantic probe loses accuracy and VLM escalation on disagreement cases recovers it.

\subsection{Generalized K-Stage Formulation and Three Cascade Cases}
\label{sec:kstage}

The proposed detector is a special case of a $K$-stage cascade. Let
$f_1, \ldots, f_K$ denote detectors with non-decreasing cost
$C_1 \le C_2 \le \cdots \le C_K$ and accuracies $A_1, \ldots, A_K$ on a
fixed evaluation distribution. The useful quantity is the conditional gap
on the inputs that stage $i$ actually routes forward. The global gap
$A_{i+1}-A_i$ is only a coarse summary. Escalation improves accuracy only
when the next stage is better on that routed subset.

In our setting, let $K=3$: $f_1 = f_{\text{art}}$, $f_2 = f_{\text{sem}}$, and
$f_3 = f_{\text{vlm}}$. The routed subset is the disagreement set
$D = \{x : \hat y_{\text{art}}(x) \neq \hat y_{\text{sem}}(x)\}$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:disagree}). The resulting cascade accuracy is
\begin{equation}
A_{\text{cascade}} = (1-p_D)\, A_{\text{agree}} + p_D\, A_{3\mid D},
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
where $p_D=P(x\in D)$, $A_{\text{agree}}$ is accuracy on the agreement set,
and $A_{3\mid D}$ is verifier accuracy on $D$. On the agreement set,
$f_1$ and $f_2$ make the same prediction by construction
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:pidisagree}). The gain over the semantic-only detector is
therefore
\begin{equation}
\text{Gain} = A_{\text{cascade}} - A_2
= p_D \left( A_{3\mid D} - A_{2\mid D} \right),
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
where $A_{2\mid D}$ is the accuracy of the semantic detector on the same routed
subset. Thus, a stronger verifier helps only if it is stronger on
disagreement cases. Average accuracy alone does not determine the cascade gain.
This leaves us with three cascade cases.

\textbf{Case 1 (both cheap stages strong, $A_1 \approx A_2 \approx A_3$).}
The artifact and semantic detectors usually agree, so $p_D$ is small. Even
a strong verifier has little effect on end-to-end accuracy because it is
rarely invoked.

\textbf{Case 2 (asymmetric cheap stages, $A_1 \ll A_2$, and $A_2 \gtrsim A_3$).}
The semantic detector is already the better cheap model. Many disagreement
cases are therefore inputs where the artifact detector is wrong and the
semantic detector is right. If $A_{3\mid D} < A_{2\mid D}$, routing replaces
correct semantic predictions with noisier verifier predictions, so
Gain $<0$. In this case, any improvement over the \emph{artifact}-only
baseline comes from the semantic detector, rather than from escalation.

\textbf{Case 3 (both cheap stages degraded, $A_1 \ll A_3$ and $A_2 \ll A_3$).}
Both cheap detectors are unreliable with disagreement being common. If the
verifier remains accurate on $D$, then $A_{3\mid D}$ can substantially
exceed $A_{2\mid D}$. This is the cascade case where Eq.~\eqref{eq:gain} becomes
large and positive, and the gain is caused by routing hard cases to the
verifier.

\textbf{Breakdown condition.} Disagreement is useful only when the two cheap
detectors fail differently and the verifier is better on the routed subset.
If $f_1$ and $f_2$ share a correlated blind spot, they can agree while both
are wrong. Those inputs never enter $D$, so the cascade cannot route them to
the verifier. In the extreme, $p_D \to 0$ and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:decomp} reduces to
$A_{\text{cascade}} \approx A_{\text{agree}}$, even when the agreement set
contains systematic errors. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} maps these cases and this breakdown
condition to the empirical results in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
Section~\ref{sec:validation_gate} formalizes a validation gate that estimates, from a small
labeled calibration sample rather than full post-hoc evaluation, which of
these cases a given deployment setting falls into.

\subsection{Baseline Routing Strategies}

To evaluate the effectiveness of disagreement-based routing, we compare two alternative strategies.

\textbf{Entropy-based routing.}  
We use the entropy of the artifact detector as a measure of uncertainty:

\begin{equation}
H(x) = - \sum_{i} p_i(x) \log p_i(x),
\end{equation}

where $p_i(x)$ are the predicted class probabilities. Inputs with entropy above a threshold are escalated to the VLM. This represents a standard confidence-based routing approach.

\textbf{Learned escalation classifier.}
\label{sec:bandit_rename}
As a learned routing baseline, we train a supervised logistic-regression classifier on
features derived from detector output, including artifact detector confidence,
semantic confidence and prediction disagreement, to predict whether an input should be
escalated to the VLM. It has no exploration policy and is not trained with
reinforcement learning (details in Section~\ref{sec:learned_routing}).

\subsection{Latency Measurement}

Inference latency for each model component is measured independently using synchronized CUDA execution on a fixed hardware setup. The total latency per sample is the sum of the costs of all models invoked by the routing policy. Artifact and semantic detectors are modeled as additive costs, which is a  conservative estimate since these stages could run in parallel in practice. All latency measurements are intended for relative comparison across routing strategies rather than as absolute deployment guarantees.

\section{Experiments}

This section describes the evaluation methodology used to assess the proposed routing framework. We first define the compared methods and evaluation metrics, then summarize implementation details and latency measurements.

\subsection{Evaluation Strategy}
We evaluate our framework against artifact-only and semantic-only detectors applied without routing, an always-escalate pipeline that invokes the verifier on every sample, and entropy-based and learned-classifier routing baselines. UnivFD~\cite{ojha2023fakedetect} is included
as a published 2023 baseline using CLIP ViT-L/14 features trained on GAN images. We
evaluate two verifiers: Qwen2.5-VL-7B~\cite{bai2025qwen25vltechnicalreport} as the
baseline and GPT-5.5~\cite{openai2026gpt55} as the primary, to test whether a stronger verifier better exploits the disagreement signal.
Evaluation focuses on detection accuracy, balanced accuracy, 
average computational cost, and escalation rate. For video 
datasets, a representative frame is sampled from each clip 
and treated as an image detection task following standard 
evaluation practice~\cite{wen2025busterxmllmpoweredaigeneratedvideo}.



\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

The pipeline integrates three heterogeneous detection components. The first stage is an artifact-based detector. The primary artifact detector is a dual-stream frequency-domain probe that combines radial FFT magnitude profiles~\cite{dzanic2020fourier} with DCT high-frequency statistics~\cite{frank2020leveraging, Corvi_2023_ICASSP} and color channel
coherence features, trained as a logistic regression classifier. Each image is resized
to $256{\times}256$ and converted to grayscale for the first two streams, giving a
43-dimensional feature vector: (i) a 32-bin radial profile of the log-magnitude of the
centered 2-D FFT (mean over each normalized-radius bin), L2-normalized; (ii) eight
statistics (mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis, and the 5th, 25th, 75th and
95th percentiles) of the bottom-right $3{\times}3$ coefficients of every non-overlapping
$8{\times}8$ block DCT; and (iii) the three pairwise Pearson correlations between the R,
G and B channels. Features are standardized and classified with
\texttt{LogisticRegression(C=0.1, max\_iter=2000, random\_state=42)}.
CNNSpot~\cite{wang2020cnn} is retained as an artifact detector baseline. The second stage is a semantic detector. Visual embeddings are extracted with one of three backbones: CLIP
ViT-B/32~\cite{pmlr-v139-radford21a}, SigLIP2~\cite{tschannen2025siglip2}, or
DINOv2~\cite{oquab2023dinov2}. It's then passed through a logistic regression classifier trained on the dataset's training split, the full pool minus its fixed evaluation set (Section~\ref{sec:exp_setup}). Every detector uses the same split and evaluation protocol. The third and final stage is an optional VLM verifier which is only invoked 
on disagreement cases.


To utilize GPT-5.5 as the primary image verifier, we prompt it as follows:

\noindent

\vspace{10pt}
\end{minipage}

genuine photographs. We replaced these

and which match the real-image source used to train the SD~1.4 semantic probes.

Table~\ref{tab:real_image_sources} reports the full cascade under the original PASCAL VOC

substitution changes absolute accuracy but not the direction of the result. Under PASCAL

general; this particular benchmark carries a confound that affects any detector.

Confidence scores are derived from the logistic probe output for the FFT artifact detector and all semantic detectors. For the CNNSpot baseline, confidence is the maximum softmax probability from the ResNet output. The learned escalation classifier and entropy baselines use these scores as features. The disagreement rule uses only binary predictions and requires no confidence
calibration.

The routing policy operates on the joint output of the artifact and semantic detectors.
In the disagreement threshold configuration, escalation is triggered whenever the two first-stage detectors produce conflicting
predictions. In the entropy threshold configuration, escalation is
triggered when the artifact detector's prediction entropy exceeds $\tau = 0.5$. The
learned escalation classifier (a logistic-regression router,
Section~\ref{sec:bandit_rename}) is trained on artifact
confidence, semantic confidence, and their disagreement signal to produce
a binary escalation decision.

We measure deployment latency on an NVIDIA RTX A4500 GPU with synchronized CUDA execution. The FFT probe takes 2.5~ms per sample. Each semantic detector takes 10.4~ms per sample,
giving a cheap-stage latency of 12.9~ms. This represents a 4.4$\times$ reduction in cheap-stage cost compared to the CNNSpot-based configuration (56.2~ms). A GPT-5.5 call averages roughly 3--5~s, including its hidden reasoning pass, and a Qwen2.5-VL call 71--190~ms in-domain; token cost is reported in Section~\ref{sec:cost_accounting}. Latency comparisons assume sequential FFT and semantic detector execution. Parallel
execution would further reduce cheap-stage cost.

\subsubsection{GPT-5.5 and Qwen Verifier Audit}
\label{sec:gpt55_audit}

Every GPT-5.5 call uses the \texttt{gpt-5.5} model identifier via the OpenAI Responses
API, with \texttt{reasoning\_effort}, \texttt{verbosity} and \texttt{temperature} left at
the API defaults. Because \texttt{gpt-5.5} spends output tokens on hidden reasoning before
answering, the 16--100-token cap of an earlier version of our code produced empty or
truncated responses (e.g.\ \texttt{"FA"}), which that code mapped to a fixed label. The
corrected implementation escalates the output budget from $2{,}048$ to $4{,}096$ tokens
over up to 5 retries, accepts a response only if it is exactly \texttt{"REAL"} or
\texttt{"FAKE"}, and records anything else as missing. We corrected 19 SDXL/FLUX
responses that the old logic had mislabeled. GPT-5.5 coverage is complete (100\%) for
every result reported here; the uniform-escalation and real-image-source runs used the
OpenAI Batch API (18{,}501 calls, 18{,}490 valid on first pass, the rest recovered synchronously), and the last 375 Qwen sample-backbone pairs of
Table~\ref{tab:e8_results} were scored locally with the original prompt. Calls made since
the correction carry UTC timestamps, retry counts and raw response text in the released
per-sample files; for earlier calls only file modification times are available.
The size-matched control (Section~\ref{sec:sizematched}) adds two further uniform runs
over the same 7{,}315 images, at 512 and 256 pixels, with the same pipeline and settings
on 2026-10-22: 14{,}630 calls, every one returning a valid answer. Batch requests that
failed for lack of account credit were resubmitted unchanged, and 4 requests that
stalled in the batch queue were completed through the synchronous path.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section presents empirical results across video, diffusion, and GAN benchmarks. We first describe the datasets and evaluation protocol, then compare detector performance, analyze routing behavior, and finally study the robustness of the proposed signal through ablations.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp_setup}

We evaluate on three in-domain benchmarks spanning distinct generator families.
GenBuster-200K-mini~\cite{wen2025busterxmllmpoweredaigeneratedvideo} comprises 1{,}000
samples from four modern AI video generators and 1{,}000 real video clips.
GenImage SD~1.4~\cite{NEURIPS2023_f4d4a021} comprises 6{,}000 Stable Diffusion v1.4
images and 6{,}000 real ImageNet images. GenImage
BigGAN~\cite{NEURIPS2023_f4d4a021} comprises 6{,}000 BigGAN images and 6{,}000 real
ImageNet images. Each sample is represented by a single frame or image following standard
practice~\cite{wen2025busterxmllmpoweredaigeneratedvideo}.

We additionally evaluate under deployment shift on two modern diffusion benchmarks.
Fake images are drawn from AIGIBench~\cite{li2025artificial} SDXL and FLUX1-dev
subsets. Real images are ImageNet val/nature photographs from the GenImage SD~1.4
partition. AIGIBench's bundled PASCAL VOC real images are replaced because image size alone separates them from the fakes; results on them and on an independent COCO source are reported alongside (Section~\ref{sec:implementation}, Table~\ref{tab:real_image_sources}). Each benchmark contains
4{,}000 samples (2{,}000 fake, 2{,}000 real). All semantic probes are trained
exclusively on SD~1.4 data. No SDXL or FLUX images appear in any training split.


Each in-domain dataset has two non-overlapping, stratified splits: a fixed evaluation set (1{,}000 samples for GenBuster, whose full pool is 2{,}000; 2{,}000 each for SD~1.4 and BigGAN, from 12{,}000-sample pools) and a training set holding every remaining sample. All detectors (the FFT probe, CLIP, SigLIP2, DINOv2 and CNNSpot) are trained on the same training split and evaluated on the same held-out samples. No nested validation split is used, since the fixed logistic-regression classifiers ($C=0.1$) need no early stopping. Split identifiers are released with the code, and every number in this paper, including those given only in the text, is released in one results file (\texttt{paper\_results.xlsx}) that maps it to the script and data producing it. Exact-path checks confirm that no image is shared between any dataset's training and evaluation splits.
\label{sec:real_image_dedup}
Real images are reused across benchmarks, however: all of SDXL's and FLUX's real images come from the 6{,}000-image SD~1.4 real pool, and 31.5\% (SDXL) and 33.1\% (FLUX) of SD~1.4's evaluation-set real images also appear in the SDXL/FLUX real class. This does not affect any detector's own train/evaluation separation, but it makes the two benchmarks' real-class difficulty correlated. The overlap counts are released with the split identifiers.

We compare FFT-only, CNNSpot-only~\cite{wang2020cnn}, UnivFD~\cite{ojha2023fakedetect},
and semantic-only configurations using CLIP~\cite{pmlr-v139-radford21a},
SigLIP2~\cite{tschannen2025siglip2}, and DINOv2~\cite{oquab2023dinov2}. Routing
baselines include entropy threshold ($\tau = 0.5$), disagreement-based escalation to Qwen2.5-VL-7B or GPT-5.5, and uniform escalation to Qwen2.5-VL. All configurations
are evaluated under identical conditions on the same held-out splits.

Accuracy figures report point estimates unless confidence intervals are shown. Balanced accuracy is the mean of fake and real recall. Latency
is measured in milliseconds on an NVIDIA RTX A4500 GPU.

\subsection{Detector Performance}
\label{sec:detector_performance}


\textbf{Artifact detectors.} Table~\ref{tab:main_results} compares the dual-stream
FFT probe against CNNSpot across all three in-domain benchmarks. FFT is more accurate
and substantially faster on every dataset, with its largest accuracy advantage on
BigGAN.

\textbf{Semantic detectors.} Among the three semantic probes, SigLIP2 achieves the
strongest in-domain accuracy on GenBuster and is effectively tied with CLIP on BigGAN.
CLIP is marginally ahead on SD~1.4, with substantially overlapping confidence intervals.
We use SigLIP2 as the primary semantic backbone for consistency across evaluated
conditions. All three probes have the same measured latency.

In-domain semantic-only inference provides the strongest low-cost operating point. Qwen escalation stays below the leading semantic probes because Qwen is weaker on the routed subsets, which is \textbf{Case 2} in the K-stage formulation (Section~\ref{sec:kstage}). On GenBuster the routed inputs are not intrinsically difficult for every cheap detector: the semantic probe remains 96.1\% accurate on this disagreement subset (Table~\ref{tab:verifier_subset}). Disagreement here mainly flags inputs where the artifact detector degrades while the semantic detector stays reliable, and escalating to a weaker verifier replaces a correct semantic prediction with a less reliable one.

Although the disagreement cascade outperforms the FFT baseline on GenBuster and
SD~1.4 (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}), this gain comes from replacing the
artifact detector with the stronger semantic detector on disagreement cases.
It should not be interpreted as evidence that VLM escalation is helping in this
setting. Escalation becomes useful only in Case 3, where the verifier is stronger
than the cheap detectors on the routed subset.

\textbf{Verifier comparison.} Table~\ref{tab:verifier_subset} compares each verifier with the best cheap detector on the disagreement subset it would receive. In-domain, no verifier clears that bar: GPT-5.5 is significantly worse than SigLIP2 on GenBuster, and its small edge on SD~1.4 is not significant. Qwen2.5-VL-7B stays below the best cheap detector on all three benchmarks. Whether a configuration is Case~2 or Case~3 therefore depends on the verifier, the detector pair and the dataset.

\textbf{UnivFD baseline.} UnivFD~\cite{ojha2023fakedetect}, trained on GAN images using
CLIP ViT-L/14, fails on GenBuster and SD~1.4 but remains effective on BigGAN. This
contrast shows that backbone strength alone does not overcome a mismatch between the
detector's training artifacts and the target generator family.

\subsubsection{Size-Matched Control}
\label{sec:sizematched}

Every SDXL and FLUX fake is a $1{,}024{\times}1{,}024$ PNG, while the ImageNet real
images are smaller JPEGs of varying shape. A classifier given only image width and height
separates real from fake with 98.9--100.0\% accuracy under each of the three real-image
sources, the same kind of shortcut found for BigGAN (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), and
GPT-5.5 receives several times more input tokens for a fake than for a real image. We
therefore center-cropped every image to a square, resized it to $256{\times}256$
(bicubic) and saved it as PNG, so both classes are downscaled and share dimensions and
format, and repeated the evaluation unchanged: the same stale SD~1.4 probes and a new
uniform GPT-5.5 run with the same prompt and settings, in which every image costs the
same 119 input tokens (Table~\ref{tab:sizematched}).

The cascade's gain over the stale probe remains significant in all four cells, and
GPT-5.5 still beats the best cheap detector on every disagreement subset, so the Case~3
assignment is unchanged. To check that disagreement picks useful inputs rather than
merely escalating many, we compare the cascade with sending a random subset of the same
size to GPT-5.5. Disagreement routing is better in three of the four size-matched cells
(FLUX/DINOv2 is not significant), as in all four cells on the native images. The margin
over random routing is small, 0.4--2.3 points across native and size-matched images, so
most of the cascade's gain under shift comes from sending a large share of inputs to a
much stronger verifier, not from which inputs disagreement selects. GPT-5.5
itself used the size cue to some extent: always-GPT-5.5 accuracy falls by 2.6--3.3
points, mostly on real photographs. Because escalated images no longer cost more tokens
than the rest, the cascade costs 39--47\% of always-GPT-5.5.

A 512-pixel version of the control upscales almost every real image while downscaling
every fake. The cascade's gain over the stale probe stays significant in all four cells
there as well (3.4--7.6 points); only the comparison with random routing depends on the
image size chosen. The upscaling drives the stale FFT probe below chance on both datasets,
mostly by labeling real images fake, and disagreement then no longer beats random
routing in any cell, so we use 256 pixels, where neither class is upscaled; the
512-pixel results are in the released results file. No resizing removes the real
images' original JPEG compression history.

\subsection{Routing Analysis}
\label{sec:routing_analysis}

In this section we analyze whether artifact-semantic disagreement between the FFT artifact detector and
the semantic probe provides a reliable routing signal, and how selective escalation
affects the cost-accuracy tradeoff across in-domain and deployment-shift conditions.

With FFT+SigLIP2, disagreement routes 25.9\% of GenBuster inputs, 26.6\% of SD~1.4
inputs and only 0.7\% of BigGAN inputs, where FFT is already near ceiling. Most inputs therefore stay on the cheap agreement path. The rate rises to 36--42\% under the SDXL and FLUX shifts, a larger candidate subset that the verifier may or may not improve.

When the FFT artifact detector and semantic probe agree, the agreement subset achieves
96.8\%, 97.8\% and 99.8\% accuracy on GenBuster, SD~1.4 and BigGAN.
When they disagree, FFT accuracy on the disagreement subset drops to
3.9\% on GenBuster and 21.6\% on SD~1.4. On GenBuster, SigLIP2 stays 96.1\% accurate on the same subset, so disagreement there flags artifact-detector failure; on SD~1.4 the two detectors degrade together. Disagreement therefore flags inputs where \emph{a} cheap detector is unreliable, but which detector fails, and whether a verifier can do better, differs by dataset.

On GenBuster with FFT+SigLIP2, 249 of FFT's 273 errors produce disagreement and are
escalated, as are 10 of its 727 correct predictions. The remaining 24 errors are shared by both detectors, the rule's blind spot: correlated failures cannot be detected through disagreement.

By generator on GenBuster (released results file), the cascade improves on FFT for four of five groups, but SigLIP2 alone beats the cascade on every generator, again Case~2.


The learned-escalation-classifier comparison is reported in Section~\ref{sec:transfer} and interpreted in
Section~\ref{sec:learned_routing}.

Selective routing reduces latency substantially relative to uniform Qwen invocation
(Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). In-domain, the semantic-only baseline remains faster and
more accurate than escalation, separating routing efficiency from verifier utility.

\textbf{Joint confidence-routing baseline.} The entropy baseline in Table~\ref{tab:main_results} thresholds only the artifact detector's confidence. As a stronger comparison, we escalate when $H_{\text{joint}}(x) = \max\big(H(p_a(x)),\, H(p_s(x))\big)$, the binary entropy of the less confident cheap probe, exceeds $\tau$. We set $\tau$ to the $(1-\hat{p}_D)$-quantile of $H_{\text{joint}}$ on a validation split, where $\hat{p}_D$ is the disagreement rate on that split, so both rules target the same escalation rate. In-domain, the validation split is a seeded 50\% sub-split of the probe-training pool; for SDXL and FLUX, which have no training split, $\tau$ is fit on SD~1.4 with the same stale probes and frozen. Non-escalated inputs keep the semantic prediction, and escalated inputs receive the same verifier labels as the disagreement rule.

The joint rule's strength under shift comes mainly from falling back to the semantic probe; if non-escalated inputs keep the more confident probe's prediction instead, it loses accuracy. Disagreement is therefore not a more accurate signal than calibrated confidence in general. Its advantages are practical: it needs no threshold, calibration data or probability outputs, and in no configuration is it more than 1.7 points less accurate than the calibrated joint rule.

Two further points follow from Table~\ref{tab:confidence_routing}. First, the benefit of escalating under shift does not hinge on the routing signal: with GPT-5.5 as verifier, confidence routing improves on the stale semantic probe by 3.6--7.7 points across the four SDXL/FLUX configurations, close to disagreement's gain, so the cascade and its gate apply with either signal. Second, disagreement supports a decision a confidence score
does not. For a single input, disagreement cannot say which of the two detectors is
wrong. With a labeled calibration sample, however, the disagreement subset shows
\emph{which} cheap detector is failing there, because it compares an artifact detector
with a semantic one. The gate's three-way selection (Section~\ref{sec:threeway}) uses
exactly this, and the case analysis of Section~\ref{sec:three_cases} rests on it.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity to Semantic Probe Quality}

The disagreement signal assumes that both cheap detectors provide useful predictions.
When one detector falls to near-random accuracy, disagreement no longer identifies hard
cases reliably. Cross-family experiments show that semantic probes trained on one
generator family transfer poorly to distant families, with off-diagonal performance
remaining close to chance regardless of source domain.

This limits how the cascade should be deployed. Applying it to a new generator family
requires an in-domain semantic probe, or at least a probe that remains meaningfully above
chance on the target distribution. The disagreement rule adds no trainable parameters,
but it still depends on both cheap detectors that carry a usable signal.

\subsubsection{Distant Generator Shift: BigGAN}
\label{sec:biggan_distant_shift}

SDXL and FLUX are diffusion models from the same broad lineage as SD~1.4, the dataset
used to train the stale FFT and semantic probes. To test a more distant shift, we apply
the SD~1.4-trained FFT and SigLIP2 probes zero-shot to GenImage BigGAN, a GAN-based
generator. This differs from the cross-family deployment experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:cross_family_deployment}), where FFT is trained in-domain on the target dataset. Here, both cheap
detectors are stale, matching the SDXL/FLUX deployment-shift setup.

The BigGAN shift degrades both cheap detectors more sharply than SDXL or FLUX. On the
disagreement subset, however, stale SigLIP2 remains strong while stale FFT collapses.
This is an asymmetric Case~2 pattern rather than the joint degradation needed for
Case~3.

We evaluate GPT-5.5, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (4-bit quantized) on a
stratified random sample of 1{,}500 disagreement cases. All three verifiers produce a 
negative cascade gain relative to the stale semantic probe (Table~\ref{tab:verifier_subset}, last block).
None exceeds stale SigLIP2 on the routed subset, so escalation replaces reliable
semantic predictions with weaker verifier outputs; the same holds when GPT-5.5 and
Qwen2.5-VL-7B are rerun on upscaled inputs (below). This matches the Case~2 failure mode
from the cross-family experiment (Section~\ref{sec:cross_family_deployment}).

The SDXL/FLUX finding therefore does not extend automatically to a distant generator family. At native resolution all three verifiers are strongly biased toward ``real'' on this subset, whose fakes are natively $128{\times}128$ and give a VLM little to inspect.

\textbf{Resolution test.} To test whether input size drives this bias, we resized every image so that its longest edge is 512 px, with nothing else changed. This raises GPT-5.5 and Qwen2.5-VL-7B substantially, mostly through fake recall (Table~\ref{tab:verifier_subset}, 512\,px rows; Qwen2.5-VL-72B does not fit on our GPU and was not rerun), and Qwen2.5-VL-7B then outranks GPT-5.5. Because the verifiers' ranking changes with input size, we draw no conclusion about model scale. In every run, no verifier approaches the stale semantic probe on the routed subset, so escalation hurts under this shift.



\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section interprets the empirical results presented in Section V. We first examine
why artifact-semantic disagreement flags inputs where a cheap detector is unreliable, then map the
three K-stage cascade cases from Section~\ref{sec:kstage} onto the empirical results and assess whether
learned routing improves on the disagreement signal, then characterize the role of the
semantic probe and VLM verifier under in-domain and deployment-shift conditions, and
finally discuss generator distribution shift, limitations, and practical deployment
guidelines.



\subsection{Artifact-Semantic Disagreement as a Routing Signal}

The complementarity arises from the signal types each detector uses. The FFT probe
captures spectral regularities introduced during synthesis, including upsampling grids
and DCT coefficient patterns. Semantic probes encode high-level scene content learned
from natural image-text distributions. These signal types are largely orthogonal.
Agreement indicates that these independent cues support the same label, while
disagreement exposes reduced reliability without calibrated confidence. The severity of
that reduction varies by dataset, so disagreement identifies candidates for escalation
without establishing that a verifier will improve them.

\subsection{The Three Cascade Cases in Practice}
\label{sec:three_cases}

The K-stage formulation (Section~\ref{sec:kstage}) predicts three qualitative cascade cases, and
the empirical results exhibit all three. \textbf{Case 1} occurs on BigGAN in-domain.
The artifact detector is already near ceiling, so the cascade adds latency with almost
no accuracy change (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). That near-ceiling accuracy is largely
a native-resolution shortcut in the benchmark rather than generative-artifact detection
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), so this instance of Case~1 reflects the benchmark as
much as the detector.

\textbf{Case 2} occurs when the cheap detectors are asymmetric. This describes the
in-domain GenBuster and SD~1.4 results (Section~\ref{sec:detector_performance}), and appears more clearly in five of
the six cross-family pairings (Table~\ref{tab:cross_family_deployment},
Section~\ref{sec:cross_family_deployment}). A weak transferred probe makes the cascade appear useful against the
stale baseline, while comparison with the stronger cheap detector removes that apparent
gain. The BigGAN distant shift is another Case~2 instance because the semantic probe
remains stronger than every tested verifier on the routed subset.

\textbf{Case 3} occurs on the SD~1.4-to-SDXL/FLUX shift with GPT-5.5 as verifier
(Table~\ref{tab:e8_results}). Both cheap detectors degrade, and GPT-5.5 is stronger on
each routed subset. Replacing it with Qwen makes every gain negative, showing that the
case assignment depends on the full detector-verifier configuration. Two smaller Case~3
instances appear elsewhere: the BigGAN$\to$GenBuster cross-family pairing and the
probe-degradation sweep's SigLIP2 probe at 1\% training data.

A capable verifier does not by itself produce Case~3. In the BigGAN distant-shift
comparison (Table~\ref{tab:verifier_subset}, last block), all three verifiers, including GPT-5.5 and
the 72B Qwen model, remain far below the stale semantic probe on the routed subset.
Weak cheap detectors are not enough either; the verifier must exceed the stronger cheap
detector on the routed subset, as Eq.~\eqref{eq:gain} requires.

The probe-degradation sweep (Table~\ref{tab:limited_data}) demonstrates the
Case~2-to-Case~3 transition within a controlled experiment. As the semantic probe
weakens, verifier escalation changes from harmful to useful. The absence of the same
transition on GenBuster confirms that relative routed-subset strength, rather than the
training fraction itself, determines the case. Table~\ref{tab:case_mapping} counts a
sweep row as Case~3 only when the verifier beats the \emph{best} cheap detector on the
disagreement subset with a paired-bootstrap 95\% CI above zero. Several rows of
Table~\ref{tab:limited_data} show a positive cascade gain over the semantic probe without
meeting that bar, so they count as Case~2.

The \textbf{breakdown condition} occurs when both cheap detectors share a blind spot.
Such errors remain invisible to disagreement-based routing by construction
(24 of 1{,}000 GenBuster inputs, Section~\ref{sec:routing_analysis}), and the agreement-path audit in
Section~\ref{sec:validation_gate} quantifies how often this occurs across every
evaluated dataset, not only GenBuster.

\subsection{Limitations of Learned Routing}
\label{sec:learned_routing}

The learned escalation classifier is a supervised logistic regression over 11
detector-derived features: the FFT probe's confidence, margin, entropy and logit, both
probes' predicted labels, SigLIP2's probability, margin and entropy, the disagreement
indicator, and the gap between the two probes' probabilities. Its training label marks
whether escalating would have been better for that sample, scoring each action as $+1$
if correct and $-1$ if wrong minus a small latency penalty ($\lambda{=}0.05$ times
normalized latency). There is no reward feedback at deployment and no exploration. It gives no consistent improvement over binary disagreement, which suggests that whether the two detectors agree already captures the dominant routing signal (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}).


\subsection{Role of the Semantic Probe}
\label{sec:role_semantic}

The role of the FFT detector in the cascade is diagnostic, not predictive. In agreement
cases, the FFT and semantic probe produce identical predictions by construction. Any
routing policy that escalates only disagreement cases to the VLM reduces to outputting
the semantic probe's prediction on agreement cases unchanged. The FFT detector
 does not contribute to independent prediction on these samples. It serves as a cheap signal
whose agreement with the semantic probe identifies easy inputs, routing only the
residual disagreement cases to the VLM.

This role makes the cascade most useful when the semantic probe becomes stale while the
verifier remains strong. Cross-family results also show that a parameter-free routing
rule still depends on useful cheap-detector predictions; a new generator family may
require an in-domain probe.

\subsection{VLM Domain Sensitivity}

Using the prompt in Section~\ref{sec:implementation} with REAL/FAKE logit extraction, Qwen2.5-VL achieves
more reliable binary predictions when classification uses the REAL/FAKE logit gap rather
than parsed output tokens. Greedy decoding can bias the textual response toward
``REAL'' even when the underlying logits separate the classes. This is a methodological
consideration for VLM-based binary classification beyond this detector.

Even with logit extraction, Qwen remains weaker than the trained semantic probes
in-domain. Selective invocation improves on uniform Qwen use, though it still does not
surpass the semantic-only baseline. Routing can therefore improve how a weak verifier is
used without making it the best available model.

Qwen's routed-subset accuracy varies substantially across settings. In several
cross-family pairings it falls below the in-domain FFT baseline, explaining why
escalation does not reliably beat the stronger cheap detector. Verifier value is therefore
distribution-dependent.

\subsection{Generator Distribution Shift}

The contrast between CNNSpot and FFT suggests that artifact representation matters as
much as detector class under generator shift. CNNSpot's GAN-specific cues transfer
poorly to diffusion and video generators~\cite{Wang_2023_ICCV}, while the FFT probe's
spectral representation transfers more broadly. Artifact robustness alone still does
not determine cascade value because the routed verifier may remain weaker than the
semantic probe.




\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

One validity concern is the real-image source used in the SDXL/FLUX deployment-shift
evaluation. The real half of these benchmarks uses ImageNet val/nature photographs
rather than the bundled AIGIBench PASCAL VOC images (Section~\ref{sec:implementation}). This means the real
images come from the same source as the real images used to train the SD~1.4 semantic
probes, while the fake SDXL/FLUX images remain out of distribution. Table~\ref{tab:real_image_sources} therefore also reports the original PASCAL VOC images and an independently chosen source (COCO val2017).

Under escalation, both real-class and fake-class recall increase in all four SDXL/FLUX cells, so the cascade's gain is not confined to the fake class; the confusion matrices, computed from the same probe snapshot and complete GPT-5.5 coverage as Table~\ref{tab:e8_results}, are released.

Correlated cheap-detector errors affect 2.4\% of GenBuster inputs and 8.2--16.1\% of the SDXL/FLUX agreement path (Section~\ref{sec:validation_gate}), so the routing signal's fundamental limit is non-negligible, and larger evaluation sets would strengthen the generalization claims. The learned escalation classifier is
trained and evaluated within the same benchmark distribution, so its generalization
to unseen generator types remains untested. Qwen2.5-VL is applied zero-shot without
fine-tuning. However, a fine-tuned VLM may recover the domain sensitivity failures observed on
SD~1.4 and BigGAN. The study uses frame-level detection without temporal modeling,
and incorporating motion and frame consistency could improve performance on
video-specific generators. All results, including those on the GenBuster video benchmark, concern frame-level detection; temporal video forensics was not evaluated. The semantic probe does not transfer across generator
families and deploying this cascade on a new family requires retraining the probe on
in-domain data. The disagreement rule itself has no trainable parameters and needs
no retraining. The SDXL/FLUX analysis excludes CLIP because the raw SD~1.4 features
required to reconstruct the stale probe were unavailable.

A further limitation concerns the artifact detector's near-ceiling in-domain accuracy on BigGAN (99.7\%, Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). BigGAN's fakes are natively $128{\times}128$ pixels without exception in a 300-image sample, while the real ImageNet images span over 300 native resolutions. A classifier given only width and height reaches 99.55\% on the same split, and forcing the real class through the downsampling the fakes undergo before the shared $256{\times}256$ resize drops the same trained FFT detector to 50.1\%, exact chance. The detector is also nearly invariant to JPEG compression (99.65\% at quality 50) but collapses under blur and downscale-upscale round trips (29.2\% at $\sigma{=}2$), which points to a coarse resize signature rather than generative texture. SD~1.4 shows a partial version of the confound, with a genuine signal remaining, and GenBuster, whose classes share one native resolution, shows none. Two cross-family pairings (GenBuster$\to$BigGAN and SD~1.4$\to$BigGAN) use this in-domain BigGAN FFT detector as the comparator that beats the cascade, so its strength should be read with the confound in mind.

Across all three cheap detectors (released results file), FFT is the most fragile under blur, rescaling and JPEG compression, while the semantic probes degrade far less. Because disagreement routing depends on the artifact detector, such perturbations would raise the disagreement rate and move more inputs to the verifier.

The SDXL/FLUX benchmark carries a comparable size shortcut. Its deployment-shift result
survives a size-matched control (Section~\ref{sec:sizematched}), but that control cannot
remove the real images' original JPEG compression history, and always-GPT-5.5 accuracy
falls by 2.6--3.3 points under it, so part of the verifier's apparent strength on the
native images also comes from image size.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

Deployment should begin with the strongest cheap detector and treat disagreement as a
candidate escalation trigger. If a semantic probe is accurate in-domain, direct probe
inference is preferable. Under shift, escalation should be enabled only after the chosen
verifier demonstrates an advantage on a labeled sample from the routed subset. This
policy separates the identification of difficult inputs from the question of whether a
particular verifier can resolve them.

\subsection{A Practical Fail-Safe: the Verifier Validation Gate}
\label{sec:validation_gate}

A deployed system may not know whether an incoming distribution is in-domain, a benign
generator update, or a larger cross-family shift. A natural first option is to use the
disagreement rate as a shift signal. This is insufficient: the cross-family and
SDXL/FLUX ranges overlap despite opposite escalation outcomes, while the BigGAN distant
shift remains close to the in-domain rate even though every tested verifier hurts
performance there.

We therefore propose a \textbf{verifier validation gate}. Before allowing escalation for
a verifier $V$ on a new target distribution, evaluate $V$ on a small labeled calibration
sample drawn from the disagreement set, and freeze the resulting decision before
applying it to new, unlabeled inputs.

\textbf{Decision rule.} Let $\hat A_1$, $\hat A_2$ and $\hat A_3$ be the accuracies of the
artifact detector, the semantic probe and the verifier on the labeled calibration sample.
The binary gate either escalates or falls back to the stronger cheap detector,
\begin{equation}
g_{\text{bin}} =
\begin{cases}
f_3, & \text{if } \hat A_3 > \max(\hat A_1, \hat A_2),\\
f_{\arg\max_{i \in \{1,2\}} \hat A_i}, & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:gate_binary}
\end{equation}
and the three-way selector picks the most accurate of the three detectors,
\begin{equation}
g_{\text{3way}} = f_{\arg\max_{i \in \{1,2,3\}} \hat A_i}.
\label{eq:gate_threeway}
\end{equation}

As decision procedures the two rules select the same action in every case, because the binary gate's fallback branch is $\arg\max(A_1, A_2)$, so the binary rule also chooses between the artifact and semantic detectors on fallback. They differ only in what the evaluation below records: the binary analysis checks the escalate/fallback decision against the oracle, while the three-way analysis checks the full three-valued choice, which explains the one place their reported numbers diverge ($N{=}10$).

We use a prespecified, uncertainty-aware version of the escalate condition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:gate_binary}: escalate only if the paired-bootstrap 95\% CI (10{,}000 resamples) on $\hat A_3 - \max(\hat A_1, \hat A_2)$, computed on the calibration sample, lies entirely above zero, so a small gap that could be sampling noise does not trigger escalation.

The gate is evaluated on the same 35 verifier-on-disagreement configurations as the baseline comparison of Section~\ref{sec:baseline_comparison}.

\textbf{Agreement-path audit.} Calibration on the disagreement subset cannot establish the accuracy of the agreement path, where no verifier is consulted, so we audited it separately on every dataset. In-domain, agreement-path accuracy is 96.8--99.8\% and correlated errors (both detectors agree and are wrong) affect 0.2--3.2\% of agreement-path inputs. Under the SDXL/FLUX shift, accuracy drops to 83.9--91.8\% and correlated errors rise 4--8$\times$, to 8.2--16.1\%. These errors are invisible to a gate calibrated on disagreements; they are the breakdown condition of Section~\ref{sec:kstage} under deployment shift.

\subsubsection{Independent (Held-Out) Gate Evaluation}
\label{sec:gate_holdout}

An oracle computed from the \emph{same} pool used for calibration measures resampling stability, not generalization, so we also evaluated the gate under genuine calibration/test separation. For each configuration and $N \in \{50, 100, 200\}$, we drew 250 random splits of the disagreement subset into disjoint calibration and test sets of $N$ samples each, plus 250 half/half splits of the full pool, capping $N$ at half the pool where necessary (129 for GenBuster at $N{=}200$). The gate's decision, under both the naive and the significance-aware rule, is frozen on the calibration set and applied unchanged to the test set. We record agreement with the test set's own oracle, regret against the best fixed detector, harmful escalations (escalating although the verifier is below the best cheap detector on the test set) and missed beneficial escalations (falling back although the verifier is strictly best). Two configurations with pools under 40 samples (BigGAN/Qwen2.5-VL-7B and BigGAN/GPT-5.5) cannot be split, leaving 33.

The held-out agreement counts in Table~\ref{tab:gate} are below the same-pool counts, confirming that the same-pool figures measured resampling stability. $N{=}100$ gives reliable decisions in only about two-thirds of eligible configurations and $N{=}200$ in about three-quarters, so practitioners should budget closer to $N{\approx}200$ or use the full calibration pool, and should expect some close-margin configurations to remain unreliable.

The two rules make a different trade. The significance-aware rule cuts harmful escalations by roughly an order of magnitude (7.3\% vs.\ 0.3\% at $N{=}50$) but escalates less, so it misses more beneficial escalations and has higher mean regret, and with the half pool it matches the test oracle less often than the naive rule. It suits deployments where an unwarranted escalation is costly, and the naive rule suits those where a missed verifier gain is.

The last panel of Table~\ref{tab:gate} shows how often the significance-aware gate enables escalation on the four SDXL/FLUX GPT-5.5 configurations. Three escalate in nearly every draw, but FLUX/SigLIP2, where the verifier's margin is smallest, needs about 200 labeled samples before the gate reliably keeps its gain.

\subsubsection{Extending the Gate to Three-Way Selection}
\label{sec:threeway}

The three-way rule calibrates the accuracy of $f_1$ (artifact), $f_2$ (semantic) and $f_3$ (verifier) on the same labeled sample and selects the best for subsequent disagreement inputs.

The oracle analysis shows that no single detector dominates across
deployment conditions: it selects the semantic probe in 17 of the 35 configurations, the
verifier in 10 and the artifact detector in 8. The artifact detector is preferred in five of the six cross-family pairings (Table~\ref{tab:cross_family_deployment}). The binary gate's fallback makes the same choice there; the three-way framing reports it as an explicit selection among all three detectors.


With a realistic labeled batch, the three-way selector tracks the binary gate's calibration reliability across the sweep (Table~\ref{tab:gate}, same-pool rows).

\subsection{Cost Accounting and Cascade vs.\ Uniform Escalation}
\label{sec:cost_accounting}

To compare the cascade with both a standalone cheap detector and a uniform always-escalate policy, we ran GPT-5.5 on every SDXL and FLUX image through the OpenAI Batch API (\$2.50/\$15.00 per million input/output tokens for gpt-5.5~\cite{openai_pricing}, half the synchronous price): 8{,}000 calls, all valid on first pass. A call used on average 783 input tokens on both datasets and 96 (SDXL) or 177 (FLUX) output tokens; on the in-domain disagreement subsets the averages were 1{,}271/251 (GenBuster), 335/215 (SD~1.4) and 192/184 (BigGAN) input/output tokens.

The last three columns of Table~\ref{tab:e8_results} report the comparison. Uniform escalation reaches 99.6\% accuracy on SDXL and 97.1\% on FLUX, while the cascade costs 42--51\% of that while escalating 36--42\% of inputs. The cost share exceeds the escalation share because the cascade is priced from the tokens of the images it escalates, which use more tokens than the average image. The cascade leaves 3.0 to 7.8 percentage points of the uniform policy's accuracy unrecovered, so selective escalation is a cost-accuracy tradeoff point, not a free improvement over always escalating.

At this scale the uniform policy is affordable (Table~\ref{tab:e8_results}). The cascade earns its place when volume or latency is the constraint: it cuts multi-second verifier calls by 58--64\% and dollar cost by 49--58\%, which matters at millions of images per day, under API rate limits, or when a self-hosted verifier's GPU throughput is the bottleneck.

Dollar figures cover GPT-5.5's API cost only and use the token counts recorded per call; Qwen2.5-VL runs locally and is reported as latency, using the constants measured once on the hardware of Section~\ref{sec:implementation}.

\section{Conclusion}

This work frames AI-generated media detection as an adaptive model selection problem. A two-stage cheap pipeline routes inputs through an FFT artifact detector and a semantic probe and escalates to a frontier VLM only when the two detectors disagree. Three research questions were evaluated with this design.

For RQ1, artifact-semantic disagreement consistently identifies a subset with reduced
cheap-detector reliability, though the severity varies by dataset. It cannot see errors
both detectors share, and those rise 4--8$\times$ under deployment shift.

For RQ2, the disagreement rule needs no threshold or trained parameters to select
inputs, but deciding whether to escalate them still requires labeled calibration data
(the validation gate). A learned escalation classifier over the same detector outputs gives no consistent advantage. A calibrated joint confidence rule is less accurate than disagreement on SD~1.4 and BigGAN in-domain but tied or better under the SDXL/FLUX shift, and the cascade's gain under shift holds with either signal; disagreement delivers it with no threshold or probability outputs. With a labeled
calibration sample, its disagreement subset also shows which cheap detector is failing
there, which is what the gate's three-way selection uses.

For RQ3, disagreement-based escalation reduces verifier cost, but its accuracy benefit is
conditional. It recovers accuracy under the SDXL and FLUX shifts when paired with a
strong verifier, including when real and fake images are size-matched, yet fails in five of the six cross-family pairings and under the distant BigGAN shift.
Escalation should be enabled only when calibration confirms that the verifier exceeds
both cheap detectors on the routed subset.

The effective deployment of heterogeneous detection pipelines depends on the use of cheap detectors to identify when expensive verification is needed rather than increasing the complexity of the individual detector. All experiments evaluate isolated frames, so the results concern frame-level detection rather than temporal video forensics. Future work will explore temporal modeling for video detection, fine-tuned VLM verifiers for domain-specific forensic cues, and cross-family semantic probe training to remove the per-family retraining requirement.
