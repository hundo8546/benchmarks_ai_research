IEEE ACCESS PEER REVIEW — REVIEWER 1 COMMENTS

Paper: On Reliable Detection of AI-Generated Multimedia Content
Authors: Ansuman Nayak, Vijay K. Madisetti

================================================================================

REVIEWER COMMENTS:

(1) The problem can be discussed in a systematic way:

The problem discussed in the paper can be generalized as a system optimization 
problem. There are three heterogeneous detectors (artifact detector D1, semantic 
detector D2, and a VLM verifier V1) and one routing strategy in the system. For an 
input dataset, each detector has associated compute cost (say C1, C2, Cv, 
respectively) and accuracy (say A1, A2, Av). Usually, C1, C2 << Cv. Theoretically, 
A1, A2 << Av; however, this assumption is not always true (for Qwen2.5 in-domain 
case) in this paper. 

The paper claimed:
(a) the cascaded scheme can improved the accuracy for the out-of-domain input 
    with less token cost;
(b) the "disagree threshold" routing policy is simpler and as good as the 
    "learned bandit" policy.

The authors should discuss every scenario with experiment evidence to support the 
claims in a systematical way. For two cheaper detectors, here are three combinations:

Case 1 (both detectors show high accuracy): escalation is rarely triggered; the 
system maintains high accuracy at low cost.

Case 2 (one detector is strong, the other is weak/fair): The routing scheme 
primarily flags cases where the weaker detector fails but the stronger one succeeds. 
As seen in the in-domain experiments, the semantic probe is highly accurate while 
the FFT probe is fair. Thus, the "disagreement gain" may largely stem from the 
performance gap between these two detectors combined with the performance of the 
VLM.

Case 3 (both detectors show low accuracy, unreliable): Under deployment shift, both 
detectors may degrade.

I recommend the authors should explicitly formalize these three cases, putting 
together with experiment results and discussions, to give a clear concept about 
when the cascade scheme works and when it fails.

(2) The fundamental value proposition of the cascade framework should rely on the 
assumptions that C1, C2 << Cv and A1, A2 << Av. If A1 or A2 > Av, the high-
accurate detector should be adopted, no need to escalate to verifier. The framework 
is only justifiable if the cascade scheme can achieve the accuracy that 
A(cascade) >= max (A1, A2, Av) while maintaining low cost, C(cascade) is a bit 
higher than C1+C2.

In this paper, the experiment setup shows a distinct discrepancy between the 
assumptions. When using Qwen2.5 as verifier for in-domain test data, from Table 3, 
4, 5 the A(Qwen) is lower than A(FFT-only) and A(SigLIP2-only). The cascade scheme 
(in Table 3) shows the accuracy drops from 0.985(SigLIP2-only) to 0.905 
A(disagree->Qwen-FFT-SigLIP2).

(3) Complete the baseline columns in Tables:

To verify the exact source of the cascade's performance gains, it is critical to 
see the individual accuracy of all detectors side-by-side with the final cascade 
accuracy. I found several tables didn't show these details:

Table 7 & Table 8: suggest adding the columns of accuracy for FFT-only on SDXL/FLUX 
and Qwen2.5-only on SDXL/FLUX alongside the GPT-5.5 metrics.

Table 9: suggest adding the columns of accuracy for SigLIP2 and Qwen2.5.

Table 18 (Cross-Family Deployment): Only the Semantic-Stale and Cascade results 
are listed. The performance of the FFT-only detector must be added. Given that 
FFT-only achieves strong results in Tables 3, 4, and 5 (e.g., 0.753, 0.748, 0.998), 
it is mandatory to find out whether the accuracy of cascade comes from disagreement 
or just the accuracy gap between two detectors.

For out-of-domain test, we may be able to evaluate where the major contribution 
comes from when these columns are added.

(4) Unfair comparison in Table 15 vs. Table 16:

In Table 16, it was generated with the newly proposed FFT + SigLIP2 + Qwen 
configuration to achieve high accuracy. The authors claim that this disagreement 
threshold outperforms the Bandit transfer baselines in Table 15. However, the 
metrics in Table 15 were generated using the legacy CNNSpot + CLIP baseline. This 
is an unfair comparison since the gains might simply come from the superior 
foundational capabilities of FFT/SigLIP2 rather than the routing framework itself. 
The authors must update Table 15 using the same FFT + SigLIP2 backbone to ensure 
a rigorous comparison.

(5) For the Case 3 (both detectors are weak):

If both detectors are unreliable, is the disagreement signal still a valid flag for 
difficulty, or does it degenerate into a random trigger? I suggest the authors 
should provide experiment data or give a clear mathematical note defining the 
breakdown point of the disagreement signal when entering Case 3.

(6) In real-world applications, the system does not have prior knowledge of whether 
an incoming sample is "in-domain" or "out-of-domain" relative to the training 
distribution. A truly reliable detector framework must be fail-safe under all 
circumstances. In the proposed cascade framework, there is no guarantee to get 
highest accuracy, the worst case may happen. I suggest the authors may consider a 
practical decision-making guideline to ensure a reliable result for any input 
distribution.

(7) The manuscript currently contains an overwhelming number of tables, which 
severely hinders readability. I suggest consolidating or restructuring the data 
presentation to make the core takeaways easier to digest.
